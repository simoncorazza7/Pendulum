PART 1The rig and the task

Hardware

  • Cart on an MGN15 linear rail, pulled by a GT2 belt over a 60-tooth pulley (120 mm of belt per motor turn) by a NEMA 23 stepper through an A4988 driver at 24 V. The driver's microstep pins choose the step size: 1/8 step gives 1600 steps per motor turn, so 13 333 steps per metre (75 µm per step).
  • Pendulum: a threaded rod with a weight on the shaft of a quadrature encoder (600 pulses/rev). Counting both edges of both channels gives 2400 counts per revolution, 0.15° per count.
  • Two limit switches near the ends of the roughly 0.5 m track.
  • A Raspberry Pi 4 runs everything: it reads the encoder and switches on GPIO pins and drives the driver's STEP, DIR and EN pins. EN high means the driver is off and the motor unpowered.
  • The Pi gets 5 V from the same 24 V supply, so the rocker switch (today's emergency stop) cuts motor and Pi together.

What we can measure and what we can command

A stepper is a kinematic source: as long as it doesn't lose steps, the cart moves exactly as commanded, whatever its mass or the belt friction. So the input to the model is the cart acceleration a, and the main way the actuator can fail is step loss: the cart silently falls behind the step count. It can't be seen directly; you find it by driving to a switch and comparing where it triggers with where it should.

The task

  • Stage 0: bring the rig up safely, audit the inherited thesis code, measure everything the controller depends on, and build a simulation that reproduces the rig. Gates A and B are review points where the project stops for a decision.
  • Stage 1: an angle-only PID running entirely on the Pi catches the pendulum when it is released near upright and holds it there. No swing-up and no cart-position feedback: the cart may drift until a soft limit stops it. Gate C closes Stage 1.

PART 2Background theory

Equation of motion

Angle θ from upright, positive when the top leans toward +x, pivot on the cart. For a rigid pendulum with mass m, distance l from pivot to centre of mass, inertia J about the pivot and viscous pivot friction b, with the pivot accelerated at a:

Pendulum on an accelerating pivotJ θ̈ = m g l sin θ − m l a cos θ − b θ̇

Dividing by J and writing ω₀² = m g l / J:

θ̈ = ω₀² sin θ − (ω₀²/g) a cos θ − (b/J) θ̇

Two numbers describe the whole pendulum: ω₀, how fast gravity acts, and b/J, the friction. Mass and shape only enter through them. The equivalent length Leq = g/ω₀² is the length of a point-mass pendulum that behaves the same: measured ω₀ = 4.931 rad/s, Leq = 0.40 m.

The broom on your palm. If the top leans right, gravity makes it fall further right, unless you accelerate your hand to the right and push the bottom back under it. That is why the controller's gains are positive: lean toward +x, accelerate toward +x.

Hanging versus upright

Near hanging (θ = π + φ) the equation becomes a stable oscillator with a period of 2π/ω₀ = 1.27 s. A free swing therefore measures ω₀ (from the period) and b/J (from how fast it dies out). The sign of the input flips at the bottom: accelerating toward +x makes a hanging bob lag toward −x.

hanging: φ̈ = −ω₀² φ − (b/J) φ̇ + (ω₀²/g) a upright: θ̈ = +ω₀² θ − (b/J) θ̇ − (ω₀²/g) a

Near upright an uncorrected tilt grows like eω₀t: it multiplies by e ≈ 2.7 every 0.2 s. That is the speed the controller has to beat, and why the loop runs at 500 Hz, a hundred samples per growth time.

The controller: PID on the angle

a = Kp · θ + Ki · ∫θ dt + Kd · θ̇
  • P pushes the cart under the pendulum in proportion to the tilt. It has to beat gravity: Kp > g, or the cart can't accelerate fast enough to get underneath. This is the lower stability limit.
  • D reacts to the angular velocity and damps the motion; without it the loop oscillates.
  • I integrates the tilt. It removes a steady angle error and, as shown below, acts as the controller's hidden memory of the cart's velocity.

Closing the loop on the linear model gives a third-order system. Pole placement chooses the gains so the closed loop has a damped pair of poles at ωc with damping ζ and a real pole at p:

Pole placement(s² + 2ζω_c s + ω_c²)(s + p) Kp = g (1 + (ω_c² + 2ζ ω_c p) / ω₀²) Kd = (2ζ ω_c + p − b/J) · g / ω₀² Ki = g ω_c² p / ω₀²

ωc is the speed of the closed loop, faster than the instability ω₀. ζ = 0.7 gives a well-damped response. p sets how quickly the integral acts; p = 0 is a PD controller. Proposed: ωc = 2ω₀, ζ = 0.7, p = 0.5ω₀, giving Kp = 62.8, Ki = 96.7, Kd = 6.56 in SI units. Kp means 1.1 m/s² of cart acceleration per degree of tilt, or 0.16 m/s² per encoder count.

From the ideal model to the real loop

The continuous model puts no upper limit on the gains. The real loop does, because of:

  • Sampling and delay: the controller acts every 2 ms on slightly old information (computation, step thread, driver).
  • Quantisation: the angle is known only to 0.15°.
  • Saturation: the cart can only accelerate and move so fast. These limits (amax, vmax) are measured in step 0.9.

The standard tool for "how close to unstable" is the Nyquist criterion. With one unstable pole in the plant, the loop is stable only if the open-loop curve L(ejωT) circles −1 exactly once counter-clockwise. From it come the margins: how far all gains can be scaled down or up, how much phase is to spare where the loop gain crosses 1, and how much extra delay the loop tolerates. Computed exactly on the sampled model with the real filter: gain ×0.25 to ×6.9, phase margin 50°, delay margin 48 ms. The delay margin matters most for later stages, where the loop may run over a network.

Estimating the angular velocity

The D term needs θ̇, but the encoder only gives counts. The simplest estimate, the backward difference (change in angle per 2 ms), jumps by 2.6 mrad / 2 ms = 1.3 rad/s whenever a single count changes. A low-pass filter spreads that spike out, but every filter adds lag, and lag eats delay margin.

Each count step adds the same velocity Kd · q ≈ 17 mm/s to the cart command whatever the filter; the filter only decides whether it arrives as a tall short spike (risking clipping and step loss) or a low wide bump. On real recordings the 2nd-order Butterworth low-pass at 20 Hz halves the closed-loop command noise of the first filter and cuts the spikes 2.4×, for 6 ms of delay margin.

Where upright is: calibration without an index

The encoder counts from 0 at program start, so the angle reference has to be found again at every start. Two physical facts make it possible:

  1. Hanging puts the centre of mass exactly below the pivot, upright exactly above it. The two are exactly 180° apart, whatever the pendulum's shape.
  2. A free swing oscillates symmetrically about the hanging rest position, so fitting a damped sine to the encoder edge times finds its centre to a fraction of a count.
u(t) = c + e^(−σt) (a cos ωt + b sin ωt), upright = c + 1200 counts ω(A) = ω₀ (1 − A²/16) (a larger swing is slightly slower)

Fitting the edge crossings (exact positions at exact times) instead of sampled counts, and correcting the frequency for the swing amplitude, keeps the centre within about 0.13 counts. The next section shows why that precision matters.

Why the cart drifts: a conserved quantity

Integrating the upright equation once gives a quantity no controller can change (friction neglected):

The drift identityv(t) − v(0) = g · ∫θ dt − L_eq · (θ̇(t) − θ̇(0)) after a catch: v_∞ = v(engage) + L_eq · θ̇(engage) + g · ∫θ dt
  • PID: the closed loop forces the integrator back to zero, so ∫θ dt → 0 and the cart returns to its engage velocity plus the pendulum's momentum term. The integrator is hidden cart-velocity feedback.
  • PD: the ∫θ of the catch stays as cart velocity (simulated: 68 mm/s after a 3° catch).
  • Reference error: if true upright is off from the controller's zero by δ, the pendulum can only stay up while the cart accelerates at a = g · δ: about 26 mm/s² per count of error, which reaches the 0.15 m soft limit in 3.4 s.
  • A hand on the rod after engage breaks the identity: the controller accelerates the cart while the hand stops the pendulum from reacting, and that momentum stays. About 90 mm/s for every 50 ms of holding.
  • Quantisation: the ±1-count jitter settles at the count boundary nearest upright, up to half a count off, worth up to ±6 mm/s² of drift. The trim only partly removes it.

Angle-only control cannot stop all drift; it only keeps the cart's velocity memory. Holding the cart in place needs cart-position feedback, a later stage. Stage 1 accepts the drift, measures it and minimises it.

Identifying the plant

  • Free decay of the hanging pendulum: the period gives ω₀; the shape of the decay separates viscous friction (exponential) from Coulomb friction (linear). Measured b/J = 0.024 1/s and Coulomb 0.0117 rad/s², tiny next to the controller's own damping (about 14 1/s).
  • Input gain: the model says a cart acceleration a changes θ̈ by (ω₀²/g)·a. A short known pulse with the pendulum hanging measured 93 % of that, and its sign confirmed both sign conventions together.
  • Closed-loop check: the hanging damping test should make a swing die out at the designed rate. Measured 1.019 1/s against 0.986 designed, an input gain of 103 %.

PART 3Implementation

Two programs

  • main, the thesis code: a TCP server that drives the stepper and reports the angle. Kept working because other tools use its protocol, with safety fixes: it no longer homes or powers the motor at start, and signal handlers switch the driver off.
  • pendctl, the new controller runtime: one program with modes (encoder-log, move, shadow, damp, and later balance). Every mode that can touch the real pins refuses unless explicitly allowed, and by default talks to a simulated GPIO chip instead.

The control loop, every 2 ms

Layers

The same controller code runs in a fast simulation on the PC and on the rig, so it is tested thoroughly before it ever moves anything, and a second implementation in Python catches errors in either.

Core

Pure functions with no input/output and no clock: PID, supervisor, units, velocity estimator, swing fit. Each has a Python twin; golden files make the C++ reproduce the Python results (bit-identical on the PC, within 10⁻¹⁵ on the Pi).

Runtime

One control period: read, convert to SI, estimate, supervise, control, command. The balance runtime, the damping runtime and the start-up calibrator. Tested in closed loop on the simulated rig.

Hardware interface

Raw units only: counts, edge timestamps, steps. Three implementations: the real rig (PiHw), sensors only with the motor pins never claimed (SensorHw), and the simulated rig (SimHw, validated against real runs).

Modes

The real-time loop, console output and logging around a runtime. Tested on the simulated GPIO chip before the rig.

Config and logs

Every number lives in config/pendctl.conf with its unit and source. Every run writes each tick to CSV plus metadata (git commit, the exact config used).

Real time on the Pi

The Pi runs normal Linux with a preemptible kernel. Three threads get fixed real-time priorities and their own CPU cores, and memory is locked so nothing pages out mid-loop.

ThreadPriorityCoreJob
Step thread903toggles STEP at the right moments
Encoder reader852reads timestamped edge events from the kernel
Control loop801the 500 Hz tick
Everything elsenormal0

Measured: the loop wakes up 6 µs late at the 99th percentile when idle, 35 µs under full CPU load, 0.33 ms at worst; step pulses are within 12 µs at the 99th percentile. The 2 ms period has plenty of margin, so no extra microcontroller is needed.

The encoder and actuator paths

Encoder. The kernel timestamps every edge on the two channels. A dedicated thread reads them, merges the channels in time order and decodes each edge into ±1 count. It keeps the running count and the recent edges with their exact times. Each control tick takes the new edges. An impossible transition or a lost edge is flagged, and the supervisor stops.

Actuator. The thesis's step thread turns a commanded acceleration into a speed and emits STEP pulses. Its quirks (rates counted in STEP toggles, two per step; an inverted internal speed sign; its own position and switch logic) are handled in one conversion layer, so the new supervisor owns all limits.

The supervisor

The soft limit is what ends a normal Stage 1 run: the supervisor brakes as soon as the cart's position plus the distance it needs to stop (including the axis's own acceleration cap and a reaction time) reaches ±0.15 m, well before the switches.

Safety engineering

  • Driver off by default. EN is held high from boot; every program switches it off on exit, on a signal and on a crash.
  • No motion without stating the motion envelope first for that specific run: direction, distance, speed, acceleration, duration, how it ends and how to abort.
  • Simulated GPIO first. Every new mode runs on the mock chip, with the kernel's record of every pin write checked (for example "EN never went low").
  • Shadow mode runs the full controller on the real sensors with the motor pins never claimed: the safest possible real test of signs, logic and timing.
  • Build rules after day one's incident: delete binaries before building, run only after a successful build, never start anything that can open the real pins unintentionally.

The testing pyramid

  1. 63 unit tests on the PC and the Pi, including the golden comparisons with Python.
  2. Closed-loop tests on the simulated rig: catches, aborts, sign errors, delays, a hand on the rod.
  3. 21 checks of the real program on the simulated GPIO chip.
  4. Shadow runs on the real sensors, with no motion possible.
  5. Motion runs with a stated envelope and a checklist, smallest first.

PART 4The steps, one by one

Each step says what we do, what came out, and why it matters.

Stage 0: bring-up, audit, identification

  1. 0.1

    Discovery done

    Inventory of the Pi, the code and the wiring against the thesis schematic: 32-bit system, all pins free, the published code doesn't compile as is.

    Every later decision (threads, atomics, pins) depends on the actual platform, not the thesis.

  2. 0.2

    Build as is done

    Compiles with two fixes; tagged as the baseline.

    A known starting point to compare every later change against.

  3. A

    Gate A: code audit done

    A line-by-line review before running anything: 22 findings, among them uninitialised flags, data shared between threads without protection, limit switches that never stop motion, a factor-of-2 unit mix-up, a decoder that holds back the newest edge, a step thread at normal priority.

    These are exactly the bugs that turn a balancing attempt into a crash into the end stop. Finding them on paper is cheaper than on the rig.

  4. 0.3

    Safe bring-up done

    No homing and the driver off until the first command, signal handlers, protected shared data, EN high from boot, a 35-check mock harness.

    Every experiment after this one can fail safely.

  5. 0.4

    Instrumentation done

    Logging every 2 ms tick to CSV with the git commit and exact config, plus a Python analysis environment.

    Every claim in the project is backed by a log that can be re-analysed.

  6. 0.5

    Timing done

    Loop and step-pulse timing under idle and full load, plus a scope check: 35 µs late at the 99th percentile under load, no overruns. The Pi reached 82.7 °C; a heatsink is now fitted.

    The delay budget for the design, and proof the Pi alone can run the loop.

  7. 0.6

    Encoder integrity done

    Every raw edge recorded while the pendulum was turned and swung: exactly 2400 counts per turn, no lost or wrong edges in 0.7 million, no drift. One startup-loss mechanism found and fixed.

    The angle is the controller's only measurement; a miscounting encoder means a wrong upright and a cart that runs off.

  8. 0.7

    Upright reference done

    Free swings fitted to find the hanging rest position: repeatable to ±0.13 counts.

    Each count of reference error is 26 mm/s² of drift.

  9. 0.8

    Conventions and cart calibration done recheck after new PCB

    +x points to the left switch, the angle sign is +1, two STEP toggles per step. The microstep mode turned out to be intermittent: half-step one day, 1/8 the next. The new PCB fixes the wiring.

    A sign error makes the controller push the pendulum over instead of catching it; a wrong scale makes every distance, speed and acceleration wrong by the same factor.

  10. 0.9

    Actuator capability pending · PCB Wed 14 Oct

    Moves at increasing acceleration and speed with a step-loss check after each, then 500 Hz acceleration streams.

    The catch region is set entirely by how hard and fast the cart can reliably move. amax and vmax go to 50–70 % of the reliable maxima, so the controller never asks for more than the motor delivers.

  11. 0.10

    Identification done

    Free swings and a free decay give ω₀ = 4.931 ± 0.01 rad/s and the friction; the input-gain pulse gives 93 %, the damping test 103 %.

    The gains are computed from these numbers; a wrong ω₀ or input gain moves every pole.

  12. 0.11

    Velocity estimator done

    Candidates compared on real recordings and in closed loop: the 20 Hz 2nd-order filter.

    The D term is the biggest consumer of measurement noise.

  13. 0.12

    Simulation and validation done

    A nonlinear simulation with sampling, delay, quantisation, the estimator, saturation and the supervisor, plus the exact linear analysis. Then the test that counts: replaying the real pulse and damping runs. Closed-loop damping reproduced to 0.12° and 1 mm of cart travel.

    Everything Gate B claims rests on the simulation, and it is only trustworthy once it reproduces real data.

  14. B

    Gate B: design review draft ready

    Measured parameters with uncertainties, issues fixed, the proposed design with predicted margins, catch region, drift predictions and acceptance thresholds. Key finding: every candidate tuning catches the same region, so the catch region is set by the actuator, not the gains.

    At the gate, with the evidence in one place, the review decides whether the cart may try to catch the pendulum.

Stage 1: catching and balancing

  1. 1

    Shadow mode done

    The full runtime on the real sensors with the motor pins never claimed. Confirmed the calibration, the arm, engage and abort logic and the angle sign at upright; found the D-term spikes, a reader race, and that engaging on release can't yet tell a release from a hand adjustment.

    Checks everything except the motor, with zero risk.

  2. 2

    Hanging active damping done

    The first closed-loop motion: around the bottom, the cart follows the bob. Decay 1.019 against 0.986 1/s designed.

    Checks signs, delay and input gain in closed loop where a mistake only makes a swing grow, and the abort catches it.

  3. 3

    First catches after Gate B and 0.9

    Reduced amax and vmax, releases from about 2–3°.

    The first real test of the upright loop, with the least energy in play if something is wrong.

  4. 4

    Raise the limits, run the experiments next

    Catch success from 2°, 5° and 8° on both sides; balance quality; drift fits and trim iterations; PD against PID.

    Turns "it balances" into measured performance against the acceptance thresholds, and checks the drift theory on the real rig.

  5. C

    Gate C: Stage 1 report next

    Results against predictions, what limits performance, and what the next stage needs.

    Closes Stage 1 with evidence, and sets the scope of what comes after.

PART 5What surprised us

  • A failed build ran a stale program that homed the cart unasked on day one. Hence the build and run rules.
  • The step mode changes on its own. The same commanded move went 4.3× further one day than the next, from a loose microstep wire. Every motion run now starts with a measured distance check until the new PCB.
  • The first velocity filter spiked ±2.4 m/s² per count while the pendulum was held still. Found in the first shadow run, fixed with data.
  • The biggest drift source is the operator's hand, not the controller: 50 ms of holding after engage costs about 90 mm/s of cart velocity.
  • A release can't be told from a hand adjustment in the first 0.15 s from the encoder alone, so the engage-on-release logic stays off until it is tuned on labelled data.
  • Two bugs in the new code, a race in the edge reader and a clock wrap, showed up only on the Pi after every simulated test had passed. That is why the mock and shadow stages exist.
